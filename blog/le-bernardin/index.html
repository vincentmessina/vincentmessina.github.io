Before You Meet Someone You Admire

November 12, 2026

On Tuesday I met Eric Ripert. I never met any of my heroes before. I sometimes imagined this happening, like how people imagine meeting the band on their way out of a concert. It didn’t play out quite the way I had in mind.

Growing up in a family that owns a restaurant made me love the food, but more the experience of going out to eat. I see food as an avenue for expression of love, of generosity and creativity.

Sunday night my friend texted me he’d be going to New York for a work trip. He was driving and had an extra bed at the hotel he booked. I thought, great. I’ve been talking to a customer in the area; what better way to grow the relationship than going to meet him?

I’ve been reaching out to window-covering business owners for the last month, and the difference in response rate I’m getting between the Americans I reach out to and those from Canada is quite remarkable.

Americans are just more direct, and are willing to try new things. They’re more open to change. They could be much more rude, but sometimes honesty is what you need most in sales.

We made our way to New York early Monday morning. Unfortunately the hotel we stayed in didn’t have hot water, but that’s a story for another time.

I always wanted to go to Le Bernardin, but I just never had the chance. When I arrived I thought to check for reservations, but unsurprisingly they were booked up for the next two months.

I went to the New York Public Library to get some work done (incredibly magical place, by the way), and on my way back I happened to pass by the restaurant. I thought it wouldn’t hurt to walk in and ask if they had a waiting list, perhaps.

What a special feeling it was to walk in there for the first time. I think a big part of this is just that all the staff are so excited about what they are doing. You can’t pay for this kind of employee. It has to come from within. It’s incredible to be somewhere where everyone is just the best at what they do. They did confirm they were indeed full for the next two months; however, they could allow me to eat at one of the tables they set aside. My first lesson from this experience is that you can just ask for things. This is an important part of the way I think, but this is one of the best examples I’ve experienced that validates it. I was pretty excited.

I came in the next day and of course everything was incredible: the thinly pounded yellowfin tuna with foie gras was unforgettable. My favourite part is that they just aren’t pretentious. They don’t have anything to prove. They’ve held three Michelin stars in New York City for 21 years. They don’t need to use foam on every dish or any other gimmick of that sort.

Chef Ripert was in that day. He was looking around the dining room and went to talk with one of the servers. Shortly after, Chef Ripert looked at me and came over to say hi.

I thought he would appreciate it if I told him that I was grateful to be here and have this experience. But he didn’t respond right away. So I continued, feeling like I needed to explain more, and I said thanks for keeping it relatively affordable. He joked: “Perhaps I should charge more, then.”

When you get one chance to speak to someone you look up to, is it the right thing to tell them that their world-famous restaurant is cheap? What I said came from a place of gratitude. It appeared to me that he doesn’t see his establishment as a place for rich people, but just as the best restaurant.

My takeaway from this is that when you get a chance to speak to someone you may never get the chance to speak to again, decide on an outcome for that conversation ahead of time. In my case I just wanted to express gratitude, but when he didn’t say much, I kept talking, and I ended up slightly insulting him. I guess it could have gone worse.
